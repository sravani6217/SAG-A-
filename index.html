01 / INTRODUCTION

Meet Sagittarius A*

Understand what a black hole is before exploring its physics.

CONCEPTUAL BLACK-HOLE VISUALIZATION · NOT TO SCALE

What is Sagittarius A*?

Sagittarius A* (pronounced Sagittarius A-star) is a supermassive black hole located at the centre of the Milky Way.

How was it discovered?

Astronomers tracked stars orbiting an invisible, compact object at the galactic centre. Their motions revealed an enormous concentration of mass.

Can we see it directly?

No light escapes from inside its event horizon. Scientists study its surroundings and use radio telescopes to image the emission around its shadow.

Why is it called a black hole?

A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so strong that, once something crosses the event horizon, it cannot send a signal back to distant observers.

It is not an ordinary empty hole in space. It is a region described by an extremely curved spacetime geometry.

Remember: A black hole is not a cosmic vacuum cleaner. Objects can orbit it safely if their trajectories and distances allow it.

02 / ANATOMY

Inside the Black-Hole Environment

Explore the important regions around a simplified, non-rotating black hole.

ILLUSTRATIVE CROSS-SECTION
01

Singularity

Classical General Relativity predicts a central singularity in the ideal Schwarzschild solution. Curvature becomes unbounded there. A complete physical description likely requires quantum gravity.

02

Event Horizon

The event horizon is the boundary of no return. Once a future-directed signal crosses inward through it, it cannot escape to distant space.

03

Photon Sphere

For a non-rotating black hole, the photon sphere is at 1.5 times the Schwarzschild radius. Light can follow unstable circular paths there.

04

Accretion Flow

Gas and plasma can orbit the black hole. Friction, turbulence and magnetic processes heat the material, causing it to emit radiation.

05

Black-Hole Shadow

The shadow is a dark image feature caused by light capture and strong gravitational lensing. It is larger than the event horizon itself.

What is the difference between the event horizon and shadow?

The event horizon is a physical boundary in spacetime. The shadow is an apparent dark region seen by a distant observer. They are related, but they are not the same thing.

03 / THEORETICAL FOUNDATION

Schwarzschild's Theory

Discover how a simple mathematical solution describes gravity around an ideal non-rotating black hole.

Who was Karl Schwarzschild?

Karl Schwarzschild was a physicist and astronomer who found an exact solution to Einstein's field equations in 1916. It describes the spacetime outside a spherical, non-rotating, uncharged mass.

This solution became one of the foundations of black-hole physics. It gives us a mathematical way to understand the event horizon and gravitational effects.

SCHWARZSCHILD RADIUS
Rs = 2GM / c²

This is the radius of the event horizon for an ideal non-rotating, uncharged black hole.

G — Gravitational constant

Describes the strength of gravitational interaction.

M — Mass

The mass of the black hole.

c — Speed of light

The speed of light in vacuum.

SCHWARZSCHILD METRIC
ds² = −(1 − Rs/r)c²dt² + (1 − Rs/r)−1dr² + r²dΩ²

This equation describes the geometry of spacetime outside an ideal spherical, non-rotating, uncharged mass.

What does the metric tell us?

ds²: The spacetime interval between nearby events.

dt: Time coordinate used by a distant observer.

dr: Change in radial coordinate.

r: Distance coordinate from the centre.

dΩ²: Angular part of the geometry.

The metric tells us how distances and time intervals are measured in curved spacetime. It is the mathematical foundation for the simplified experiments in this lab.

Important scientific limitation: Sagittarius A* may rotate. The Schwarzschild solution is therefore an idealized approximation, not a complete description of the real object.

04 / GENERAL RELATIVITY

How Does Spacetime Work?

Gravity is not simply a pulling force in Einstein's General Relativity. It is connected to the geometry of spacetime.

CONCEPTUAL SPACETIME GRID · NOT A NUMERICAL GR SOLUTION
EINSTEIN FIELD EQUATION
Gμν = 8πG Tμν / c⁴

Matter and energy are related to the curvature of spacetime.

Space

Mass and energy change the geometry used to measure spatial distances.

Time

Gravitational fields affect the rate at which clocks run relative to one another.

Geodesics

Freely falling objects and light follow paths called geodesics through spacetime.

Why is spacetime important in this project?

A physical model can show the shape of a black hole, but it cannot directly demonstrate how clocks behave or how light follows curved paths.

The simulation helps us explore these effects through mathematical relationships and visual experiments.

07 / RELATIVITY

Gravitational Time Dilation

A clock deeper in a gravitational field can tick more slowly relative to a clock far away.

TWO CLOCKS · CONCEPTUAL COMPARISON
SCHWARZSCHILD CLOCK-RATE FORMULA
dτ / dt = √(1 − Rs / r)

This formula gives the clock rate for a stationary observer outside an ideal non-rotating black hole, compared with the time coordinate of a distant observer.

Move the clock closer

Distance: 5.00 Rs
Clock-rate ratio 0.894

How should you understand this?

Imagine two identical clocks. One is very far away, while another is held stationary closer to the black hole. When compared, the closer clock accumulates less time.

The formula does not describe a clock freely falling through the horizon. A stationary observer cannot remain at rest exactly on the event horizon.

Why does redshift happen?

The frequency of light measured by a distant observer is lower than the frequency measured by a stationary observer near the black hole.

This is a gravitational effect, not simply the ordinary Doppler effect caused by motion towards or away from us.

09 / QUANTUM AND THERMODYNAMICS

Black-Hole Thermodynamics

Black holes have theoretical temperature and entropy. These ideas connect General Relativity, quantum theory and thermodynamics.

01. Accretion heating

Gas moving through the accretion flow can become extremely hot and emit radiation. This is not the same as Hawking radiation.

02. Hawking radiation

Quantum field theory on curved spacetime predicts that black holes emit thermal radiation.

03. Entropy

Black-hole entropy is proportional to the area of the event horizon.

04. Energy transfer

Accretion and outflows can transfer energy and momentum into the surrounding environment.

HAWKING TEMPERATURE
T = ℏc³ / (8πGMkB)

For an ideal, non-rotating black hole.

BEKENSTEIN–HAWKING ENTROPY
S = kBc³A / (4Gℏ)

A is the event-horizon area.

Why is this important?

Black-hole thermodynamics connects gravity with quantum physics. It raises important questions about entropy, information and the fundamental nature of spacetime.

For a black hole as massive as Sagittarius A*, the predicted Hawking temperature is extremely low. Its observed emission is dominated by the surrounding hot plasma, not detectable Hawking radiation.

10 / WHY IT MATTERS TODAY

Why Study Sagittarius A* in This Generation?

Black-hole research is not only about discovering mysterious objects. It helps scientists test fundamental physics using real astronomical observations.

A natural laboratory for testing gravity under extreme conditions.

Sagittarius A* allows scientists to investigate how gravity behaves near a supermassive black hole, where ordinary Newtonian descriptions are insufficient.

01. Test General Relativity

The motions of stars near Sagittarius A* allow scientists to test predictions of relativistic gravity.

02. Understand strong gravity

Its compact environment provides an opportunity to study spacetime close to an event horizon.

03. Study accretion physics

Variable emission helps researchers investigate how hot gas and magnetic fields behave near black holes.

04. Improve astronomical imaging

The Event Horizon Telescope combines radio observatories to resolve extremely small structures in the sky.

05. Explore fundamental physics

Black holes connect General Relativity, quantum theory, thermodynamics and the information problem.

OUR CENTRAL SCIENTIFIC QUESTION

How does the extreme gravitational environment around Sagittarius A* affect light, time, matter and the motion of nearby objects?

Why use a simulation alongside a physical model?

The physical model helps visitors understand the appearance and structure of a black hole. The simulation explores physical effects that cannot be demonstrated by a stationary model: spacetime geometry, relativistic clock rates, gravitational redshift and light bending.

Together, they connect a visual representation with mathematical physics.
